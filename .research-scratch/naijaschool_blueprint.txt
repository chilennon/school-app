S E C T I O N 1
Executive Summary
Five independent studies — spanning Ogun, Anambra, Lagos, Osun and Enugu States between 2020 and 2026 — converge on the same
diagnosis of Nigeria's Education Management Information Systems (EMIS): the technology is not the bottleneck. The assumptions
baked into the technology are.
Every prior system — federal NEMIS, state platforms like Ogun's DiPER, and countless school-level tools — was designed around
infrastructure Nigerian schools don't reliably have (servers, desktops, stable power, broadband) and workflows that treat data entry as an
extra chore rather than something that happens automatically as teachers do their normal jobs. The result, confirmed across every paper
reviewed here: data that takes 9–12 months to become usable, enrollment figures that are wrong by up to 37%, and infrastructure audits
showing 0% server availability and 0% internet connectivity at the local-government level in Anambra State.
Yet the same body of research also proves the upside is enormous. Ogun State's DiPER platform — the one system in this research that
got the architecture right — took data accuracy from 58% to 93%, cut reporting time by 89%, and cut policy-intervention time by 45%,
simply by making data a byproduct of daily activity instead of a separate report.
The 100x opportunity: No paper reviewed here describes a system built mobile-only, offline-first, and free at the point of individual
teacher use. Every system studied assumed a desktop, a server room, or a mandate from above. The gap between "what DiPER proved is
possible" and "what's actually usable on the phone a teacher already owns, with no admin approval needed" is the entire opportunity. You
already have the right architecture in motion — this blueprint formalizes it into something that can out-perform every system in this
research by an order of magnitude, not by doing more, but by removing the dependencies that broke all of them.

2.3 What DiPER got right (and why it's still not enough)
Ogun's DiPER is the closest thing in this research to a proof of concept. It reframed data collection as a byproduct of routine activity — a
teacher's daily attendance check-in or grade entry generates the record automatically, rather than a separate report being compiled later.
That single design decision is responsible for nearly every improvement the paper documents: 127 overstaffed urban schools identified for
teacher redistribution, 43 priority sites flagged for classroom construction via GIS analysis, and 12,500 at-risk students surfaced early
enough for remedial support.
But DiPER is still a state-mandated, centrally-hosted platform layered on top of biometric hardware, RFID readers, and payment-gateway
integrations — the very infrastructure the Anambra audit shows is absent almost everywhere outside a well-funded pilot state. It proves the
model works. It does not solve distribution.

S E C T I O N 3
The Five Failure Patterns Every Past EMIS Repeats
Read across all five papers together, one pattern repeats with almost no variation. Each failure below is quoted or paraphrased from at
least two independent studies, which means it isn't a one-state problem — it's structural to how EMIS has been built in Nigeria.
1 The infrastructure assumption is wrong
Every system studied assumes a server room, desktops, stable power, and broadband will exist at the point of data entry. The Anambra
audit shows that assumption fails almost completely below the state level: 0% of local-government nodes had a server, internet connection,
UPS backup, or database backup capacity. Even at the state node — the best-resourced tier — 0% had working backup power or
database backup.
2 Digitization is mistaken for transformation
The Badagry study is the clearest evidence of this: whether records were stored in a cabinet or on a computer made no statistically
significant difference to processing efficiency. Buying computers and calling it an EMIS doesn't work if the underlying workflow — who
enters what, when, and why — is untouched.
3 The user is assumed to be technical
In Osun State, EMIS was resourced reasonably well (over 80% of respondents confirmed adequate computers, storage and skilled EMIS
officers) — and it still failed to reach its potential because only 40% of headteachers were confident enough to update their own school's
data. The bottleneck moved from hardware to human confidence, and no paper in this set treats that as a design problem to solve rather
than a training problem to schedule.
4 Data collection is treated as separate from the job
Every failing system in this research asks teachers and administrators to do their real work, and then separately report on it. Every
improvement documented (DiPER's 35-point accuracy gain) came from collapsing that gap — making the record and the activity the same
event.
5 Adoption is mandated, not earned
NEMIS, DiPER, and the state-level systems in Osun and Enugu are all top-down: a federal or state policy requires schools to use them.
Every paper independently names "negative attitudes," "resistance to digital transition," or "lack of awareness" as a leading barrier. A tool
nobody asked for, that nobody was consulted on, run by people who don't trust it — that combination shows up in five different states, five
different research teams, five different years.
The meta-lesson: Nigeria doesn't need another EMIS that tries to do what DiPER does, better. It needs a tool that gets 90% of DiPER's
outcome with 0% of DiPER's infrastructure requirement and 0% of its mandate-dependency — something an individual teacher adopts this
week because it makes their own Friday easier, with zero server, zero training session, and zero permission slip.

5.1 Non-negotiable architecture principles
Offline-first, not offline-tolerant. The app must be fully usable with zero connectivity and sync later. This directly answers the
Anambra infrastructure audit and the "constant power interruption" finding in Enugu — connectivity is the exception you design for, not
the assumption you build on.
PWA, installable, no app-store gate. Removes the biggest single-tap friction point for a teacher on a low-end Android with limited
storage. Add-to-homescreen is the install flow.
Multi-tenant from day one (already in your architecture) — one codebase, one Supabase project, Row-Level Security isolating each
school's data. This is what lets you onboard school #2 through #200 without re-deploying anything.
Progressive data richness. A school with only phones gets attendance + results + report cards. A school that later gets a front-desk
PC or tablet unlocks fee tracking, inventory, and admin dashboards — same backend, no migration.
SMS/USSD fallback for the zero-smartphone edge case. Not phase 1, but architected for: a head teacher without a smartphone can
still confirm daily attendance counts via a short SMS code, syncing into the same Supabase tables.

5.4 The "activity-based" trick, concretely
Rule of thumb: if a teacher has to open a screen whose only purpose is "reporting," the design has failed Law 4. Every data point in the
schema above is a side-effect of a screen the teacher was already going to open to do their actual job — taking attendance, entering a
score, receipting a payment. Real-time institutional data is the exhaust from those actions, never a separate destination.

approval, and it's already the strongest feature on your own CV. Everything else in the product is discovered after that first win.
